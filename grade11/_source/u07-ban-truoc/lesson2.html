Pronunciation

Rising ↗ vs. Falling ↘ intonation in questions

12 minutes
The rule

Rising ↗ vs. Falling ↘

Yes/No questions → ↗ Rising

Is the course free? ↗

Did anyone go? ↗

Is academic education important nowadays? ↗

Wh- questions → ↘ Falling

When does it start? ↘

What are your plans for the future? ↘

How long is the course? ↘

Vocabulary

5 terms for the language of educational choices

8 minutes
Vocabulary · Discover → Practise → Use

Five key terms

  • school-leaver /ˈskuːl ˌliːvə(r)/ (n) someone who has just finished or left secondary school — học sinh vừa rời trường
  • vocational education /vəʊˌkeɪʃənəl ˌedʒuˈkeɪʃn/ (n) education that trains you for a specific job — giáo dục nghề nghiệp
  • higher education /ˌhaɪə(r) edʒuˈkeɪʃn/ (n) education at a college or university — giáo dục đại học
  • qualification /ˌkwɒlɪfɪˈkeɪʃn/ (n) a certificate or degree showing you passed an exam or course — bằng cấp, chứng chỉ
  • graduation /ˌɡrædʒuˈeɪʃn/ (n) the ceremony of completing a university course — lễ tốt nghiệp

Grammar

Perfect gerund & perfect participle clause

17 minutes
Grammar · Discover

Perfect gerund — having + past participle

Form: having + V3 · negative: not having + V3

Use: refers to an action completed before the action in the main clause.

Jobs: subject of a sentence · object after certain verbs (admit, deny, regret, mention, remember…)

  • Having studied science subjects helped Mai get into engineering school. subject
  • She regrets not having chosen a more practical course. object after regret
  • He apologised for having arrived late. after preposition
Grammar · Discover

Perfect participle clause — having + V3, main clause

Form: Having + V3, main clause · negative: Not having + V3, main clause

Use: describes an earlier completed action; the subject of both clauses must be the same.

  • Having finished the course, they started looking for jobs.
  • Not having studied hard enough, his brother failed the exams.
  • Having won many competitions, she felt confident about her university application.

Gerund vs. Participle — same form, different job:

Gerund: Having won the competition made Mai feel proud. subject

Participle: Having won the competition, Mai felt proud. clause

Grammar · Task 1 · Find and correct the mistake

Spot the mistake

  1. I forgot have discussed this topic with you. have → having (perfect gerund after "forgot")
  2. Had won many maths competitions helped him win a place at university. Had → Having (perfect gerund as subject)
  3. Nam regretted not having choose a more interesting course. choose → chosen (past participle required)
  4. He was proud of had won first place in the biology competition. had → having (gerund after preposition "of")
Grammar · Task 2 · Rewrite using having + V3

Rewrite with perfect participle clauses

  1. After we listened to an introduction to the course, we asked some questions. Having listened to the introduction to the course, we asked some questions.
  2. He failed the entrance exam. He felt very disappointed. Having failed the entrance exam, he felt very disappointed.
  3. His brother had not studied hard enough, so he failed the exams. Not having studied hard enough, his brother failed the exams.
  4. After I answered the job interview questions, I felt much better. Having answered the job interview questions, I felt much better.

Wrap-up

Exit ticket

3 minutes
Exit ticket · two minutes · answer before you leave

What can you do now?

  1. What is the usual intonation of a Wh- question? Falling ↘
  2. Give a synonym of school-leaver. graduate (from secondary school) · someone who has just left school
  3. Complete: ________ the exam, she applied for several universities. Having passed
  4. What is one difference between a perfect gerund and a perfect participle clause? A perfect gerund acts like a noun (subject / object); a perfect participle clause acts like an adverb, modifying the main verb — both are formed with having + V3.
